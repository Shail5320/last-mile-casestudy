01

The problem

Every delivery app shows a countdown the moment you order. That number isn't guessed — it's predicted from patterns in thousands of past deliveries: how far the food has to travel, how bad traffic is right now, whether the rider is juggling other orders at the same time. This project builds that predictor from raw data, end to end, and explains exactly what's inside the number.

04

The math behind the number above

The live prediction at the top of this page is not a lookup — it's this exact equation, computed in your browser from the sliders you moved. No server, no API call.

ETA = 9.94 + 0.35·distance_km + 2.36·traffic + 3.30·multiple_deliveries + 10.10·festival + weather_term + city_term + vehicle_term

Each coefficient is a weight the model learned directly from the 40,188 cleaned deliveries — for example, every extra kilometer adds about 0.35 minutes, holding everything else constant, while a festival day alone adds about 10 minutes. Categorical fields like weather and city are one-hot encoded, so only one of their terms is ever "switched on" at a time.

05

Linear regression vs. random forest

Two models were trained on identical data. The simpler one is deployed above because it's fully explainable — every prediction can be traced back to a visible equation, term by term.

Linear Regression deployed above5.17 min6.480.530
Random Forest (100 trees)3.42 min4.270.795

The Random Forest explains meaningfully more variance (0.795 vs 0.530) because it can capture non-linear interactions — like traffic and festival compounding each other — that a straight-line model can't. It's documented in the notebook as the stronger model, and it's the next planned upgrade for this live demo once its internals are properly understood rather than dropped in as a black box.

06

Honest notes

Two things the model surfaced that deserved scrutiny rather than acceptance at face value.

Investigated: possible data leakage Delivery person rating came out as the single most important feature in the Random Forest — more than distance or traffic. Since ratings can be influenced by delivery speed itself, this was tested by retraining without it. Performance dropped only modestly (R² 0.828 → 0.795), suggesting it reflects genuine rider experience more than circular leakage — but it's flagged, and excluded from the deployed model as the safer call.
Small-sample coefficient The "Semi-Urban" city coefficient came out unusually large (+10.6 minutes) — larger than the festival effect. That category makes up only 141 of 40,188 rows (0.35%), so this number is likely noise from a small sample rather than a reliable real-world effect, and should be read with caution.
07

What I learned

Going in, I assumed distance would obviously be the biggest factor in delivery time — it turned out to be one of the weakest. Operational chaos (festival demand spikes, riders juggling multiple orders at once) mattered far more than geography, which wasn't obvious until I actually plotted it.

The most useful habit I picked up wasn't a modeling technique — it was learning to be suspicious of a suspiciously strong feature. When rating came out on top of the feature importance list, the right move was to test it, not celebrate it.
